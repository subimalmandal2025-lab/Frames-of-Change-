01 School & Community Context

Where We Are,
Who We Serve

Adarshanagar is a dense, working-class neighbourhood in South-East Kolkata — part of KMC Ward 108, falling under the Kasba Assembly constituency. It sits in the arc of land bounded by the EM Bypass, Ruby Hospital, and the East Kolkata Township — a zone that is simultaneously one of the city's fastest-developing corridors and home to pockets of deep socioeconomic marginality.

The children who attend Adarshanagar KMCP School do not live in the gleaming flats going up along the Bypass. They live in the lanes behind them — in cramped homes where multiple families share walls and water, where fathers drive auto-rickshaws or load trucks at the Nonadanga market, where mothers work as domestic help in the housing societies nearby. The economic geography of South-East Kolkata is one of extreme proximity between wealth and precarity, and these students navigate that gap every single day.

Adarshanagar · Kasba Constituency · KMC Ward 108 · South-East Kolkata

The Specific World These Students Come From

The shadow of the Bypass. The Eastern Metropolitan Bypass is the physical backbone of this neighbourhood — a high-speed artery lined with malls, hospitals, IT parks, and housing complexes. Adarshanagar's children see Ruby Hospital from their lanes, pass Acropolis Mall on the way to school, watch the construction cranes that never stop. They live in the engine room of South Kolkata's growth and benefit almost none of it. This proximity to visible development without access is its own particular form of dispossession.

East Kolkata Township and the wetlands. Just beyond Adarshanagar lie the East Kolkata Wetlands — one of the world's largest sewage-fed aquaculture systems, a Ramsar site, and a place increasingly threatened by construction. The local ecology is not abstract to these students. The bheries, the vegetable fields, the flooding that comes not from rivers but from inadequate drainage into a shrinking wetland — this is their environmental reality, specific and urgent.

The informal economy and mobility. This is a neighbourhood of migrants and movement. Families from Odisha, Jharkhand, and rural Bengal settled here across decades of industrial migration. Many students are first-generation urban dwellers whose grandparents came for factory work that no longer exists. The question of where you belong — between the village you're from and the city that barely acknowledges you — is one these children carry.

Nonadanga and displacement memory. The Nonadanga transit camp — a few minutes from this school — housed thousands of evicted families. Several students may have family connections to that experience. Displacement, redevelopment, and the precarity of urban tenure are not historical concepts in Adarshanagar. They are recent, local, and personal.

This context is not background material. It is the curriculum. Every film chosen, every debate question designed, every art prompt offered in this programme emerges from what these children see when they walk home from school past the Bypass, past the construction sites, past the wetland that is slowly disappearing. BTCP does not bring the world to Adarshanagar. It asks Adarshanagar's children to describe the world they already understand better than most adults who make decisions about it.

02 Problem Statement

What We're
Really Up Against

The absence of a playground at Adarshanagar KMCP is the visible symptom. The invisible disease is the systematic removal of a child's right to think, feel, argue, and create. BTCP is designed to solve both — using the Saturdays that are already ours.

🏗️

The Infrastructure Crisis

Adarshanagar KMCP has no functional playground, no library, no dedicated art or expression space. Physical space for children to explore, play, and build social bonds simply does not exist within the school. Research is clear: unstructured play and creative exploration are not luxuries — they are the developmental bedrock of confidence, cooperation, and resilience. When that space is absent, children don't just lose recess. They lose the arena for becoming themselves. We reclaim Saturday as that space.

📖

The Pedagogy Crisis

The Grade 5 classroom at KMCP schools operates on one axis: read → memorise → reproduce. The child who thinks visually, who processes through argument, who understands through story — is structurally invisible to this model. They sit in a system designed to sort them by rote performance, not to identify what they are actually capable of. This is not a personal failing of teachers, who are themselves under-resourced. It is a systemic design problem. BTCP introduces a parallel pedagogy that makes different kinds of minds visible.

🗣️

The Voice Crisis

In the five years that these children have spent in school, they have almost certainly never been asked what they think — not about a film, not about their neighbourhood, not about what kind of city they want to live in. Civic identity, critical opinion, aesthetic response, emotional interpretation: these are treated as non-subjects. The result is a generation of children who are perceptive, observant, and rich with things to say about the world — and have never been given the tools or the permission to say them. BTCP changes that every Saturday.

"These children live at the intersection of Kolkata's extraordinary cultural richness and its sharpest inequalities. They watch the Bypass grow, they see the wetlands shrink, they know what it costs when a family is displaced. They have a relationship with the world. They just haven't been invited to discuss it."

03 Why Audiovisual Learning?

The Pedagogy
of Film

Film is the world's most democratic art form. A child who cannot decode a paragraph can still interpret a scene. A student who freezes during rote recitation will argue passionately about a character's choice. Cinema bypasses the gatekeeping mechanisms of literacy anxiety and the fear of wrong answers — meeting children in the register of story, image, and emotion, which is the register they already live in.

For students at Adarshanagar KMCP, who are immersed in the visual and sonic richness of South-East Kolkata's street life — the Bypass flyovers, the bhery fishermen, the Nonadanga market, the construction noise that never stops — film is not a foreign medium. It is deeply familiar. It is the language of the shared phone screen in a household, the tea-stall television, the serial everyone's mother watches. BTCP takes that familiarity and turns it into a pedagogical act.

The film-based learning model at BTCP rests on a trifecta: the felt experience of watching a story, the verbal processing of structured debate, and the embodied making of an art response. These three modes — activated in sequence within a single Saturday session — engage the cognitive, affective, and psychomotor domains simultaneously. This is not enrichment. This is how deep learning actually happens. This is the architecture that produces confident, expressive, thinking students — not one remarkable lesson, but the cumulative effect of fifty-two Saturdays across a school year.

🌐

Universal Access to Meaning

No text-decoding required to access a film's emotional and thematic content. Every child — regardless of reading level, language background, or academic confidence — can watch, feel, and interpret. Cinema democratises the act of thinking.

🔍

Productive Ambiguity

Great films don't explain themselves. They invite multiple readings, competing interpretations, and productive disagreement. This ambiguity is not a design flaw — it is the raw material of critical thinking. There is no single correct answer to "What was this film really about?"

🎨

The Three-Mode Learning Arc

Screen (felt experience) → Debate (verbal processing) → Art (embodied making). Three modes in one session, engaging cognitive, affective, and psychomotor domains simultaneously. The SEL competencies this builds cannot be replicated by any textbook model.

🏙️

Cultural Resonance

Films about urban flooding, family stress, children navigating institutions alone, and communities fighting for basics land differently for children who live these realities. The curriculum is chosen because it resonates in Adarshanagar specifically, not generically.

✍️

Authorship as Transformation

When a child publishes an opinion about a film they watched, they stop writing for a teacher and start writing for a world that might answer back. Authorship changes a child's relationship with language — permanently, not just for a school year.

🏛️

Practising Civic Life

Children who debate publicly, chair discussions, edit a magazine, and curate an exhibition are not studying about democracy — they are practising it. BTCP doesn't produce students who know about civic participation. It produces students who have already done it.

04 Why Short Films Specifically?

The Case
for the Short

This programme uses short films — not feature films, not hour-long documentaries, not YouTube clips. The choice of the short film as the primary teaching medium is deliberate, research-informed, and specific to the context of Grade 5 learners in under-resourced KMCP classrooms.

Short films are one of the most pedagogically concentrated art forms in existence. In 4 to 15 minutes, a well-crafted short film delivers narrative tension, character development, thematic complexity, emotional stakes, and often — crucially — unresolved ambiguity. For an educator working with limited time, limited infrastructure, and students whose attention and trust are earned not assumed, the short film is the ideal vehicle.

01
Cognitive

Fits the Developmental Attention Window

Children aged 9–11 have a natural sustained focus window of roughly 10–20 minutes for narrative content. Short films (4–15 min) sit precisely inside this window — full engagement, no fatigue, complete story arc. A 90-minute feature demands infrastructure, time, and sustained attention that a Saturday session and a KMCP classroom cannot guarantee.

02
Thematic

Maximum Density, Minimum Runtime

The best short films carry more thematic complexity per minute than almost any other narrative form. A 6-minute animation about a fish navigating a flooded city can sustain a 45-minute structured debate about climate, gender, resilience, and urban life. Short films are pedagogically concentrated by design — they do not dilute their ideas across 90 minutes.

03
Logistical

Low Infrastructure, High Impact

Short films require one device — a laptop, tablet, or large-screen phone — and can be downloaded in advance for schools without reliable internet. No projector, no darkened room, no AV setup. Total download size for a curated set of 10 short films: under 500MB. This is a curriculum that works in the real conditions of a KMCP school, not an imagined ideal one.

04
Pedagogical

Ambiguity Without Closure

Unlike feature films that typically deliver narrative resolution, great short films often end open — questioning, unresolved, deliberately incomplete. This is a pedagogical asset. When a film doesn't tell students what to think, they are required to decide. That act of deciding — forming an opinion, testing it against a classmate's, defending or revising it — is the critical thinking moment BTCP is designed to create over and over again.

05
Structural

One Session = One Complete Arc

Each Saturday is self-contained. One short film generates one theme, one debate, one artwork, one published piece. Students who miss a session re-enter without having missed a chapter. The curriculum is modular — adaptable, not fragile. A rain-cancelled Saturday doesn't break the year. A film that doesn't land can be replaced. The structure holds regardless.

06
Aspirational

A Form Students Can One Day Make

Short films are not products of wealthy studios and massive crews. They are made by students, by independent filmmakers, by communities with smartphones and stories. Introducing this form to Grade 5 students is also introducing them to an aspiration: by Year 2 of BTCP, students will make their own short film. Watching becomes the preparation for making.

06 NEP 2020 Alignment

Grounded in
National Policy

BTCP is not an extracurricular enrichment programme. It is a direct, documented implementation of the National Education Policy 2020's most urgent pedagogical mandates. Every Saturday session maps to specific NEP provisions — making this programme defensible to school leadership, KMCP officials, and Teach For India stakeholders as a legitimate educational intervention, not a hobby.

NEP 2020 represents the most significant redesign of Indian education pedagogy since 1986. Its core ambition — to shift learning from rote memorisation toward competency, experiential engagement, and holistic development — is precisely what BTCP operationalises in a single-device, single-room, zero-infrastructure Saturday session. Where NEP sets the vision, BTCP demonstrates what it looks like in a KMC school in Kasba.

NEP 2020 · Para 4.6

Experiential Learning as Standard Pedagogy

NEP mandates that "in all stages, experiential learning will be adopted — including hands-on learning, arts-integrated education, and storytelling-based pedagogy — as standard pedagogy within each subject." BTCP's film-debate-art sequence is a direct, replicable, low-infrastructure delivery mechanism for this mandate at the primary level.

NEP 2020 · Para 4.7

Art-Integration as Cross-Curricular Pedagogy

NEP defines art-integration as "a cross-curricular pedagogical approach utilising aspects of art and culture as the basis for learning of concepts across subjects... embedded in classroom transactions for creating joyful classrooms and imbibing Indian ethos." BTCP's art response session is precisely this — art as a primary vehicle for processing and expressing learning, not a decorative addition.

NEP 2020 · Para 4.23

21st-Century Competencies for Every Student

NEP explicitly mandates development of "scientific temper and evidence-based thinking; creativity and innovativeness; sense of aesthetics and art; oral and written communication; critical thinking and problem solving." BTCP's SWBAT framework maps each of these competencies to specific, observable session outcomes across the full film curriculum.

NEP 2020 · Digital Education Chapter

Media as a Bridge for the Digital Divide

NEP calls for "mass media extensively used for educational purposes to address the digital divide" and "a digital repository of educational content including learning media." BTCP uses short films — minimal-device, pre-downloadable, freely accessible — as the practical, grounded response to this mandate in real KMCP classroom conditions, not aspirational lab conditions.

NEP 2020 · Holistic Development Framework

Social-Emotional Learning (SEL)

NEP recognises that holistic development must address cognitive, psychomotor, and affective domains simultaneously. BTCP's SEL framework — critical thinking, emotional literacy, civic awareness, creative voice, confident expression — maps directly to NEP's vision of the whole child. This is not the examination-performing student. It is the fully-formed human being that education is supposed to produce.

NEP 2020 · Assessment Reform

Competency-Based, Not Rote Assessment

NEP mandates assessment "aligned with learning outcomes, capabilities, and dispositions." The Citizens' Press — a monthly student-written, student-edited magazine — is exactly this: a living competency portfolio. It demonstrates real-world communication, critical argument, aesthetic judgment, and creative expression. It is observable, shareable, and community-facing evidence of learning that no mark sheet can replicate.

07 How This Changes Students

From Passive
to Published

BTCP is not enrichment. It is transformation. Each Saturday session builds a specific, observable capability — measured not by examination scores, but by what students do with the world around them. The outcomes below are not aspirational. They are structurally produced by the methodology.

🎤

Spoken Confidence

Standing in a classroom circle and saying "I disagree, and here is why" is one of the most terrifying and transformative acts a 10-year-old can perform. BTCP gives every student not just permission but a structured, repeated opportunity to do this every week. Confidence is not a personality trait. It is a practiced skill. We give it 52 weeks of practice.

🔬

Critical Thinking

Films are chosen precisely because they resist single interpretation. Students learn to hold multiple readings simultaneously, to cite specific evidence from what they watched, and to revise their position when a classmate makes a stronger argument. This is not a discussion skill. It is the cognitive architecture of a functioning civic mind.

💛

Emotional Literacy

The art session after each film asks students to respond to what they felt — not what they learned. For children who carry significant stress — economic precarity, family instability, the ambient anxiety of urban marginality — the ability to name, externalise, and process emotion through creative work is not just developmental. It is protective. SEL research consistently identifies emotional vocabulary as one of the strongest predictors of long-term wellbeing.

🏛️

Civic Identity

BTCP students do not study about the world. They form opinions about it. When a Grade 5 student from Adarshanagar writes a published essay about why the East Kolkata Wetlands should be protected — citing evidence from a film they watched and a debate they led — they have taken a step toward civic agency that no textbook lesson has ever produced in this classroom.

✏️

Authorship

The Citizens' Press is a real publication — written by students, edited by students, read by families, displayed in school. When a student's name appears above something they wrote for a real audience, their relationship with language changes permanently. They stop writing for a teacher. They start writing for a world that might answer back.

👥

Roles, Responsibility & Leadership

Debate Lead, Magazine Team, Art Team — these are not decorative titles. They are real roles with real outputs that the rest of the class depends on. A student who has chaired a class debate, curated an art exhibition, and copy-edited a magazine by age 11 has practiced the professional and collaborative skills that most adults are still acquiring in their twenties.

08 Student Showcase & Learning Events

Making Learning
Visible

The most powerful evidence of learning is what students do in public. BTCP's showcase model creates multiple high-quality moments across the school year where student thinking, art, and voice are witnessed by an audience beyond the classroom — parents, community members, school staff, and the students themselves.

Visibility is not performance for its own sake. It is what makes learning feel real and consequential. When a child knows that their debate summary will be read by their mother, their artwork displayed in the school corridor, their opinion published in a magazine that exists outside this classroom — the quality of their engagement with every session changes. They are no longer practising for an exam. They are doing something that matters.

📰

The Citizens' Press

Monthly · All Year

A student-written, student-edited monthly magazine — printed and circulated within Adarshanagar KMCP school. Real journalism. Real readership. Starting from Session One.

Debate summaries — written by Debate Leads after each session, capturing the argument, counter-argument, and class conclusion.
Student film reviews — their own language, their own verdicts. No adult edits out the strong opinions.
Artwork pages — curated by the Art Team, with each piece captioned by the student who created it.
Shared with parents and staff each month — a public, tangible record of what learning looks like in this classroom.
🖼️

End-of-Year Art Exhibition

May 2026 · School & Community

A school-based exhibition of the full year's student artwork — curated by student Art Teams, displayed in the school hall or a community space, and open to families and the Adarshanagar community.

Student-curated selection — Art Teams choose which pieces go to exhibition, write the wall labels, and design the installation layout.
Opening event with parents, school staff, and TFI representatives — one year of creative thinking made visible in a single room.
Student artists present to explain their work in their own words — the year's final, most public act of voice.
🏆

Year-End Celebration & Recognition

May 2026 · In-School

Student roles and contributions are formally recognised — not as prizes for performance, but as acknowledgements of a year of real work, real responsibility, and real output.

Debate Lead certificates — every student who opened and chaired a session debate receives a formal acknowledgement.
Bound Citizens' Press — each student receives a printed, bound copy of the full year's magazine to keep.
Best-of-Year reading — the strongest student writing from across all twelve issues, read aloud by students to an audience.
🎬

Student-Made Film

April–May 2026 · Stretch Goal

If the cohort is ready, the final sessions invite students to make a short film of their own — 1 to 3 minutes, shot on a smartphone, about a theme from Adarshanagar itself.

Student-written and student-directed — Subimal facilitates but does not direct. The story and the choices belong to the class.
Screened at the Art Exhibition — the class film plays as part of the May showcase event.
Foundation for Year 2 — if the programme continues, student filmmaking becomes a formal curriculum component.
09 One-Year Plan · June 2025 – May 2026

A Year at
Adarshanagar

This is a one-year plan, run entirely by Subimal, every Saturday at Adarshanagar KMCP School. Ten films, twelve Citizens' Press issues, one art exhibition, and fifty-two sessions across which a class of Grade 5 students become thinkers, debaters, and published writers — in one year, in one school, with one laptop and a lot of Saturdays.

Jun 2025

Launch & Community Building

Programme launch. First session: introduce BTCP, assign student roles — Debate Lead, Art Team, Magazine Team — and launch the Citizens' Press concept. First film: chosen to open a conversation about the class itself as a community and what makes a group work or break apart.

Jul–Aug 2025

Climate, Place & Environment Theme

Films screened during monsoon season, when flooding, drainage failure, and the East Kolkata wetlands are at maximum immediate relevance. First two issues of Citizens' Press published. Students write about environmental conditions they can observe from the school itself.

Sep–Oct 2025

Identity, Family & Emotional Life Theme

Films exploring family stress, parent relationships, grief, and self-image. Art Team produces the year's most personal work. One session is handled with additional facilitation care, with a private journaling option offered in place of publication for sensitive responses.

Nov–Dec 2025

School, Joy, Belonging & Empathy Theme

Films that ask what school is and what it could be — and how we treat people who are different from us. Citizens' Press mid-year special edition: students write their own vision of a school that actually sees them. Families receive this issue at a small informal showing.

Jan–Feb 2026

Dreams, Courage & Possibility Theme

Films about ambition, fear, the gap between where you are and where you want to go. Students write the most important piece of the year: "My dream. My plan." This is the piece that goes home. This is the piece parents read. This is the piece that changes what a family believes is possible.

Mar–Apr 2026

Stories, Memory & Possible Futures Theme

The power of stories to heal, to preserve memory, and to imagine alternatives. Final Citizens' Press issue in preparation. Students optionally begin writing or storyboarding their own short film about Adarshanagar — their street, their market, their wetland, their bypass.

May 2026

Showcase, Exhibition & Celebration

End-of-year Art Exhibition at Adarshanagar KMCP. Bound Citizens' Press distributed to every student. Student film (if completed) screened. All students formally recognised for their year. Year-end documentation — including session notes, student outputs, and a programme reflection — submitted to Teach For India.

10 Other Fellows · Other Schools

A Curriculum
That Travels

This programme is designed and run by Subimal at Adarshanagar KMCP for Year One. But the curriculum architecture — from film title to YouTube link, from debate question to SWBAT, from art prompt to publication template — is fully documented, shareable, and designed to be picked up by any TFI fellow or KMCP teacher with minimal preparation.

KMCP schools across Kolkata share a remarkably similar context: dense working-class neighbourhoods, absent playgrounds, overcrowded classrooms, rote-heavy pedagogy, and Grade 5 students who are deeply observant, chronically underestimated, and hungry to be heard. The specific geography changes — Sinthi, Tiljala, Garden Reach, Cossipore — but the structural conditions and the student reality are recognisably the same. What works in Adarshanagar is designed to work across that common context, with local adaptation.

How the Curriculum Works for Any Fellow or Teacher

The BTCP Film Curriculum is a plug-and-run structure. It removes the barrier of "I don't know how to do this." A fellow who has never taught through film can run a strong session on Day One because the structure does the heavy lifting. The facilitator brings presence, warmth, and their specific knowledge of their students. The curriculum provides everything else.

1
Open the Session Plan

Each film has a one-page plan: title, link, runtime, themes, SWBAT outcomes, three debate questions graded by difficulty, one art prompt, and one publication prompt. No additional prep needed to run a session.

2
Download the Film

Every film is freely available on YouTube. Each can be downloaded in advance for schools without reliable internet. Total storage for all 10 films: under 500MB — fits on any phone or laptop.

3
Run the 5-Step Structure

Screen → Circle → Debate → Art → Publish. Each step has facilitator notes, timing guidance, and a fallback prompt for when students go quiet. Flexible by design, not rigid by default.

4
Localise and Modify

Debate questions and art prompts can be adapted to any neighbourhood. The films are fixed; everything else is open. A fellow in Cossipore or Tiljala changes the "Kolkata connection" to their street. The structure holds.

The Possibility of Expansion

Adarshanagar KMCP is where BTCP begins. But it does not need to be where it ends. The curriculum, session structure, facilitator guide, film library, and Citizens' Press template developed in Year One represent a replicable system — not a bespoke experiment designed for one room. It is built to travel.

If the programme is sustained beyond May 2026, the BTCP curriculum could be offered as a shared resource to all TFI fellows placed in KMCP schools across Kolkata. Each fellow runs it independently at their school, adapting for local context while drawing on the same film library, SWBAT framework, and publication model. There is no central coordination required. There is no budget beyond a phone and internet access. There is only the structure, the films, and the Saturday.

Over time, this creates something larger: not just one school where children's voices are published and taken seriously — but a quiet network of KMCP classrooms where, every Saturday, Grade 5 students watch, argue, create, and send their ideas into a world that is slowly beginning to listen.

Expansion is not the goal of Year One. The goal of Year One is to prove it works — at Adarshanagar, with this cohort, in this city. That proof is the only argument that matters for what comes next.
